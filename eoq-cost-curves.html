Economic order quantity · cost trade-off

How much should we order each time?

Ordering in large batches means few orders but a lot of stock sitting on the shelf. Ordering in small batches keeps stock low but means many orders. The EOQ is the order size where the two costs balance and total annual cost is lowest. Drag the orange line, or change the inputs and watch the curves move.

What to notice

The balance point. The EOQ sits exactly where the holding and ordering lines cross. Setting (Q/2)·H = (D/Q)·S and solving for Q gives Q* = √(2DS/H).

Which way does it move? Raise D or S and the ordering curve lifts, so Q* moves right (order more, less often). Raise H and the holding line gets steeper, so Q* moves left (order less, more often). Press Pin curves first to keep the old picture for comparison.

Square-root effect. Doubling demand does not double the EOQ. It grows by √2, about 41%.

The flat bottom. Total cost is forgiving near Q*: TC(Q)/TC* = ½(Q/Q* + Q*/Q). Ordering 50% too much costs only about 8% extra. Ordering far too little is much more expensive than ordering a bit too much.
