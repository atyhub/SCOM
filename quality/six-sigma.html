Six Sigma Explorer

Sigma (σ) is the standard deviation: the typical spread of a process. The sigma level counts how many of those σ fit between the process mean and the nearest specification limit. Shrink the spread, and more σ fit inside the limits, so fewer units fall outside. The example is a 400 g salmon fillet pack with a contract range of 390 to 410 g.

Key ideas

σ is the process, the limits are the customer

The specification limits (390 and 410 g) come from the customer. σ comes from the process. Six Sigma improves quality by reducing σ, not by widening the limits.

Sigma level

Z = min(USL − μ, μ − LSL) / σ

How many standard deviations fit between the mean and the nearest limit. Z = 3 × Cpk.

Six Sigma = 3.4 DPMO

With the 1.5σ long-term drift, a six sigma process produces 3.4 defects per million. Without drift, it would be about 2 per billion.

Why the 1.5σ drift?

Over months, materials, people and machines shift the mean. Motorola assumed a drift of about 1.5σ, so a process that is six sigma in the short run behaves like 4.5σ in the long run.

Check your understanding

1. Set σ = 5 g with μ = 400 g. What is the sigma level, and roughly what share of packs is outside 390 to 410 g?

Z = 10 / 5 = 2. Without drift, about 4.6 % of packs are outside (45,500 DPMO): 2.3 % in each tail. That is about 27,000 bad packs a year at 600,000 packs.

2. How small must σ be for the pack line to reach six sigma (centred at 400 g)?

σ = 10 / 6 ≈ 1.67 g. The limits stay at 390 to 410 g; only the spread shrinks.

3. Keep σ = 1.67 g but move the mean to 402 g. What happens to the sigma level, and why?

Z = (410 − 402) / 1.67 ≈ 4.8. The nearest limit is now only 8 g away. Off-centre processes lose sigma level even when the spread is unchanged, which is the same idea as Cpk being lower than Cp.

4. Going from 3σ to 4σ cuts the drifted DPMO from 66,807 to 6,210. What does going from 5σ to 6σ do?

From 233 to 3.4 DPMO, about 70 times fewer defects. Each step removes most of the remaining defects, which is why the last sigmas are the hardest and most expensive to earn.
